00 Executive summary

The page a hiring manager reads first.

The 30-second version

A large operations org filed incidents through a web app that minted a ServiceNow ticket, then manually re-created the incident in Slack and emailed responders, who finally dialed into a conference bridge. The act of reporting had become slower than the act of resolving. I led design on BART — an on-call assistant living natively in Teams that turns reporting, notifying, and assembling the war-room into one continuous flow, while ServiceNow quietly stays the system of record.

What I owned
  • Reframed the brief — from "build a faster form" to "compress the first five minutes."
  • Designed the atomic unit — one adaptive card that is the alert, the status, and the door to the call.
  • Three invocation paths — bot command, message action, proactive card — so every role has a way in.
  • A governed lifecycle — workstreams, status, and a locked close that stops noise on resolved incidents.
02 Context

The team already lived in Teams. The incident process didn't.

On-call responders coordinate everything else — code, decisions, releases — inside Microsoft Teams. But the moment something broke, the process flung them outward: a web form here, a ticket there, a Slack message typed by hand, an email blast, a phone bridge. Five surfaces, none of them talking to each other, all of them between the responder and the fix.

Persona · reporter

The one who notices

First to see the symptom. Wants to raise the alarm in seconds and get back to triage — not learn a ticketing schema.

Goal: report & go
Persona · manager

The one who coordinates

Owns the response. Needs the full picture, the ability to assign work, and one place to drive status — without chasing five threads.

Goal: orchestrate
Persona · responder

The one who fixes

Pulled in mid-task. Wants to know what's wrong, what's theirs, and how to join the call — all in one tap.

Goal: act fast
03 Problem definition

The slowest part of incident response is the part before anyone responds.

Resolution is hard work that needs experts. Starting resolution should be trivial — yet it had become a relay race across tools, where every handoff added delay and a chance for something to drop. We named this the fragmentation tax: the minutes paid to coordination before a single diagnostic step is taken.

04 Research

Five findings. One of them rewrote the brief.

The brief arrived as "build a faster intake form." Before drawing a single field, I ran a process audit, stakeholder interviews across the three roles, and a teardown of the existing tools — anchored by six pointed questions sent to the client to ground the design in reality.

The six questions that grounded the work

Rather than design in a vacuum, I sent the client six requests for evidence — the actual artifacts of their current process. Every answer became a constraint the design had to honor.

Q1

The live ServiceNow flow

Every field on the current ticket — so nothing essential is lost in translation.

Q2

Workstreams

What fields, and why — the unit of parallel response work.

Q3

Conference bridges

How responders connect today (Webex) — the join we must preserve.

Q4

The email confirmation

What gets sent on ticket creation — the notification to replace.

Q5

Managers & bridges

Who manages incidents, and the full list of bridge lines.

Q6

How many roles?

Reporter and manager — the two we designed distinct paths for.

06 Solution

The whole incident — ticket, team, and call — inside one Teams conversation.

Meet BART, the on-call assistant. It doesn't replace ServiceNow; it makes it invisible. A reporter expresses intent in plain steps; BART files the ticket, posts a live card, notifies the right people, and hands everyone the same door into the war-room.

Four principles that settled every argument
01

Meet people where they already are

If the team lives in Teams, the incident lives in Teams. Zero new destinations.

02

Make the system of record invisible

ServiceNow still owns the ticket — but nobody re-keys data or opens a second tab to create it.

03

Notify with an action, not an FYI

Every alert carries the buttons to act — acknowledge, join, update — so reading and responding are one motion.

04

Design for the worst five minutes

High pressure, low patience. Smart defaults, few taps, the happy path is the fast path.

Final design · 8 screens reconstructed from the source

The end-to-end experience, inside Teams

Eight states reconstructed faithfully from the design source — catalog, bot, intake, the proactive card, workstreams, status, the locked close, and the message-action path. Each is annotated with the decision behind it. Rendered in the portfolio system — one family (Inter), light & dark, 12px floor.

01App catalog · discover BART
Decision: ship as a first-party-feeling Teams app so adoption needs no training — it appears where teams already find tools.
02Bot welcome · guided start
Decision: the first message is a capability map with one primary action — no blank input to interpret under pressure.
03Create incident · one capture
Decision: the bridge, scope, and priority are captured here, once — so the card, the notification, and ServiceNow all inherit them with zero re-keying.
04Proactive card · new incident
Decision: the alert is the workspace. Manager and workstream members are @mentioned; tapping the notification lands them right on this card.
05Workstreams · assign & track
Decision: a workstream is the unit of parallel response — owner + status + drag-to-prioritise. The manager orchestrates without a second tool.
06Change status · governed states
Decision: four governed states — New · Suspended · Service restored · Closed — keep the channel honest about whether an incident is still live.
07Closed · locked record
Decision: closing locks the thread and posts a clear banner — stale incidents stop attracting noise, and the audit record is final.
08Message action · from a thread
Decision: when a problem surfaces mid-conversation, a message action turns that exact message into an incident — context preserved, nothing retyped.

★ Leadership & influence

What I owned beyond the pixels.

Reframed the ask

Pushed back on "faster form" with a process audit, and got the client to fund the real problem — coordination, not data entry. The lollipop chart was the artifact that won the room.

Aligned three stakeholders

Reporter, manager, and the platform team each wanted a different "first screen." The service blueprint gave us one shared map to negotiate scope against.

Set the system, not the screen

Defined the incident object and its four states once, so every surface — card, dashboard, notification — inherited the same truth. That's what made the MVP buildable on the timeline.

08 Reflection

What I'd carry forward.

The strongest design move on this project wasn't a screen — it was refusing to build the screen we were asked for until we'd proven it was the wrong one. — the lesson I keep
  • Instrument it next time. The MVP shipped without telemetry on time-to-respond. I'd build the measurement into v1 so the hypothesis becomes a result.
  • The locked close earned its keep. Stakeholders doubted it; in testing it was the most-praised detail — proof that governance can feel like care, not control.
  • One object, many doors. The discipline of collapsing every entry onto a single incident object is the pattern I now reach for in any multi-surface system.
